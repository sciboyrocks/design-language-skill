# Evidence: what is actually known about logos, colour and icons

Compiled November 2026 from a source-by-source review. Use it to ground decisions and to
answer "what does the psychology say?" honestly. Samples are mostly Western students or
online panels, so treat colour meaning as the least portable finding and check it per
market.

**Grades.** STRONG: peer-reviewed and replicated, or a meta-analysis. MODERATE: a single
peer-reviewed study or a solid industry dataset. WEAK: practitioner opinion, small or
unreplicated. UNSUPPORTED: no traceable source.

**Read status** when compiled: [F] full text read, [A] abstract only, [2] secondary
write-up, [N] not opened. Re-read a source before leaning hard on a number from an [A],
[2] or [N] row.

## 1. How a mark comes to mean something

- **Exposure builds liking, modestly, then saturates.** A meta-analysis of 268 exposure
  curves from 81 articles found an inverted-U for visual stimuli (Montoya et al. 2017) [A].
  An earlier one covered 208 experiments, r = .26, peaking around 10 to 20 lab
  presentations (Bornstein 1989) [2]. STRONG.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28263645/
- **Liking also transfers by pairing.** 214 studies, d = .52; weaker when the pairing goes
  unnoticed, and it fades after extinction (Hofmann et al. 2010) [A]. STRONG. Both of
  these findings are about *feeling*, not about *meaning*.
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20438144/
- **Mark-to-function learning.** For interface icons, how closely the picture matches the
  function matters most at first; with familiarity, familiarity takes over (Isherwood,
  McDougall and Curry 2007, N = 30) [A]. MODERATE, small. This is the only direct test of
  the question found.
  https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/journal-article/11106
- **Heavy exposure does not store the design.** 1 of 85 students could draw the Apple logo
  correctly and 47% picked it out from look-alikes (Blake, Nazarian and Castel 2015) [F].
  MODERATE. Recognition and the link to the brand survive; details do not.
  https://castel.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/111/2016/11/BlakeNazarianCastel2015-AppleQJEP.pdf
- **How long it takes.** No study was found giving an exposure count for a mark becoming
  tied to a category. Closest proxy: daily behaviours became automatic in 66 days on
  average, range 18 to a modelled 254 (Lally et al. 2010) [2]. MODERATE.
  https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit

**Bottom line.** No evidence shows design alone hardwires an association, and nothing
tested it directly. Design speeds up early learning and makes the mark findable. The
lasting link comes from repeated, noticed pairing with the product doing its job. The
Spotify and WhatsApp icons are outcomes of years of use, picked in hindsight.

## 2. Descriptive versus abstract marks

Luffarelli, Mukesh and Mahmood 2019, *Journal of Marketing Research*, six studies [F,
accepted manuscript]. MODERATE overall.
https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Let_the_logo_do_the_talking_the_influence_of_logo_descriptiveness_on_brand_equity/23483705

- Logos that depict what the brand does raise evaluations and purchase intent, through
  easier processing and a sense of authenticity.
- The lift is larger for unfamiliar brands: 6.26 versus 4.41 on a 9-point scale, against
  5.02 versus 4.28 for familiar ones. The interaction itself was only marginal (p = .096,
  N = 106).
- It **reverses for products people view negatively** (palm oil 3.85 versus 4.49; olive
  oil 5.50 versus 4.78; N = 240). If the category evokes anxiety or distaste, depicting it
  literally can hurt.
- The market link is cross-sectional and small (sales beta = .13, about 400 firms) and the
  authors themselves urge caution. 59% of 597 sampled logos were non-descriptive, so
  abstract marks are the norm, not a mistake.
- No independent replication found. Critiques found are practitioner opinion [F], WEAK:
  https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/researchers-want-to-help-you-design-more-successful-logos-but-do-they-have-good-advice/
- Counter-view: the distinctive-assets school argues against leaning on meaning at all,
  since many colours or shapes could work once they are famous [N], WEAK.

## 3. Shape

- **Round versus angular.** A round logo raised perceived comfort (6.50 versus 5.35) and
  "caring"; an angular one raised durability (6.27 versus 5.39). No effect on overall
  attitude, and the effect vanished under visual distraction or contradicting ad copy
  (Jiang, Gorn, Galli and Chattopadhyay 2016; student sample, fictitious brand) [F].
  MODERATE. Shape nudges specific traits, not liking.
  https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/64985/1/Jiang_Does_Your_Company.pdf
- **Symmetry.** Symmetric logos rate higher on average; asymmetry helps only brands
  positioned as "exciting" (Luffarelli, Stamatogiannakis and Yang 2019) [2]. MODERATE.
  https://ie.edu/insights/articles/can-a-symmetrical-logo-be-bad-for-your-brand
- **What gets recognised.** Across 195 logos the best recognised were natural (they depict
  a recognisable thing), harmonious and moderately elaborate (Henderson and Cote 1998) [A].
  MODERATE. https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299806200202
- **Simple versus complex.** Simple wins early; complex gains with repetition. But
  "repetition" was 1 versus 4 showings (van Grinsven and Das 2016; N = 68 and 164) [A], so
  it says little about years of use. MODERATE.
  https://doi.org/10.1080/13527266.2013.866593
- **Implied motion** lifts engagement and attitude only when it fits the brand (Cian,
  Krishna and Elder 2014) [A]. MODERATE. https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/8436

## 4. Colour

- **Hue maps loosely to personality** (blue to competence, red to excitement), with
  saturation and value modulating it (Labrecque and Milne 2012) [N]. MODERATE.
  https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y
- **Fit matters.** Colours that match the product type and known positioning are judged
  more appropriate (Bottomley and Doyle 2006) [A]. MODERATE.
  https://doi.org/10.1177/1470593106061263
- **Match the category or differ from it? Mixed.** Across 281 top brands, differentiating
  helped in some categories and hurt in others; conforming paid where a dominant leader
  exists, especially in high-involvement categories (Labrecque and Milne 2013) [A].
  MODERATE, correlational. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:mktlet:v:24:y:2013:i:2:p:165-176
- **"Blue means trust" in finance** has no robust test; one unpublished dissertation
  reportedly found no blue-versus-green difference [N]. WEAK.

## 5. Distinctive assets: uniqueness and fame

- An asset is valuable when it is **unique** (it cues this brand and no other) and
  **famous** (most buyers know it). Uniqueness is treated as the more important of the
  two, and every new asset starts low on both (Ehrenberg-Bass Institute explainer) [F].
  WEAK as evidence, but it is the dominant practitioner framework.
  https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction
- Data: 1,281 brand elements across 13 packaged-goods categories and 19 countries.
  Characters, logos and logotypes are the most ownable; colour alone is the hardest to
  own, because competitors share it (Ward, Yang, Romaniuk and Beal 2020) [A]. MODERATE,
  and not about apps. https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jobman/v27y2020i4d10.1057_s41262-020-00187-6.html

So: pick for uniqueness first, pair a colour with a shape rather than relying on colour
alone, then never change it. Fame comes from consistent use, not from the drawing.

## 6. Icons on a crowded screen

- **Colour unlike the neighbours gives the largest search-speed effect** in a phone-like
  icon grid (Trapp and Wienrich 2018; n = 18 and 36; luminance uncontrolled) [F]. MODERATE.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6191407/
- **Complexity has lasting costs for search; concreteness mainly helps first
  comprehension** (McDougall, de Bruijn and Curry 2000) [A]. MODERATE, lab icons.
  https://staffprofiles.bournemouth.ac.uk/display/journal-article/11404
- **Concrete, simple icons are found faster** (Goetz and Neider 2024) [A]. MODERATE.
  https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/tbitxx/v43y2024i15p3676-3695.html
- Store experiments are WEAK and measure install conversion, not findability: one game
  reported +8% from a background-colour change with no method given; one news app found a
  modernised monochrome icon cut installs.
  https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2015/12/android-developer-story-sgn-game-cookie.html
  https://developer.android.com/stories/apps/spiegel-online
- Nothing traceable was found on mascots, or on glyph-on-colour versus illustrated icons.

## 7. Money and saving cues

- **No study was found testing a logo or icon cue on saving, thrift or security.**
  Nothing on piggy-bank or coin-versus-note imagery either. UNSUPPORTED. Do not claim a
  mark "makes people save".
- **In-product cues do work.** Rural India, 146 households: a photo of the saver's
  children on the savings envelope raised savings at a low target, not at a high one; two
  envelopes beat one (Soman and Cheema 2011) [F]. MODERATE.
  https://www.russellsage.org/sites/default/files/u137/jmr-C-s014-s021-online.pdf
- Kenya, randomised trial: a gold-coloured coin used to track weekly deposits more than
  doubled savings against control, beating 10 to 20% matches (Akbas et al. 2016, working
  paper) [A]. MODERATE. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:iza:izadps:dp10024

If the goal is a behaviour (saving, exercising, learning), build the cue into the product
where the behaviour happens. The icon's job is to be found and recognised.

## 8. Statistics not to repeat

All UNSUPPORTED. If the user quotes one, say so kindly and give the real finding instead.

- "Colour increases brand recognition by 80%." Traces to a page citing only a "University
  of Loyola, Maryland study"; the original claim appears to have been "up to 80%, compared
  with monochrome", about documents. https://www.colorcom.com/research/why-color-matters/
- "People decide in 90 seconds and 62 to 90% of that is colour." Asserted without data in
  a 2006 literature review; upstream credit is an institute with no published study.
- "The brain processes images 60,000 times faster than text." A two-page corporate
  brochure with no reference. https://policyviz.com/2015/09/17/the-60000-fallacy/

## 9. Case notes on famous marks

Company rationale is scarce; most "why it works" accounts are secondary. Use these as
illustrations, not proof.

| Mark | What it depicts | Kind | Colour it owns |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spotify | Three arcs in a circle, reportedly for streaming | Abstraction of the act | Green |
| WhatsApp | A handset inside a speech bubble | Object plus act | Green |
| Cash App | A currency sign (recalled; no primary source found) | The object itself | Green |
| Duolingo | An owl's face; expression changes are timed prompts | Character | Green |
| YNAB | A tree; the company gives no meaning | Metaphor | Blue-purple |
| Robinhood | A feather, after the name | Name reference | Neon accent on neutrals |
| Monzo | An M lettermark; the hot coral came from the card | Lettermark | Hot coral |
| Wise | A flag, kept and redrawn for scale | Abstraction | Green |

Two things to notice. Depicting the act or object is common but not universal: lettermarks
and metaphors also became famous. And five of the eight own the same hue, which says the
hue mattered less than owning it consistently.

## 10. What follows for a decision

1. Optimise for **uniqueness** first. Fame comes from consistent use.
2. For an unknown brand, a **light category hint** in the mark helps the cold start and
   fades as familiarity grows. Skip it if the category evokes anxiety or distaste.
3. **One bold shape plus one colour.** Fine detail is not remembered.
4. Shape nudges traits: **round reads caring, angular reads robust**. Stay symmetric
   unless the brand is positioned as exciting.
5. Choose a colour the brand can **own on the user's screen**, that still fits the
   category and culture. Colour alone is the weakest asset; pair it with a shape.
6. Build the **behaviour cue inside the product**, not into the icon.
7. Tell the user plainly: design buys speed of learning and findability. Use hardwires.
